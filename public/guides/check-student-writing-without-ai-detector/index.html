How to Check if a Student Used AI, Without an AI Detector

An AI detector looks at a finished essay and guesses. A fairer check looks at how the essay was made, and at whether the student understands it.

Why detectors are a weak basis for a decision

AI detectors give a score, not evidence. They can mark honest writing as AI-generated, especially short texts, very formal writing, and writing by students who are still learning English and use simple, predictable sentences. They can also miss AI text that was lightly edited. A score can’t be shown to a student or parent as proof of anything, and an unfair accusation can damage a student’s trust for years.

That doesn’t mean you should ignore your doubts. It means you need better evidence. Here are four kinds.

1. Look at the writing process

Real writing has a history: a slow start, sentences rewritten, paragraphs moved, pauses to think. Text pasted from a chatbot usually arrives in large blocks, with few corrections.

  • Google Docs version history shows how a document changed over time. See our guide to checking student writing with version history.
  • DraftTrail records every edit in its writing box, separates typed text from pasted text, and lets you replay the essay from the first word. It flags large pastes, very few corrections, unusually fast typing and long essays written in very little time. Each flag is a simple rule, listed on How it works.
  • Drafts and outlines: asking for a plan and a first draft before the final version is the oldest process check, and it still works.

Process evidence protects honest students most. A student who wrote for three hours can show it.

2. Talk with the student

A five-minute conversation tells you more than any score. Keep it calm and curious, not accusing:

  • “Walk me through how you wrote this. Where did you start?”
  • “This paragraph makes an interesting point. Can you explain it in your own words?”
  • “What does this word mean here?” for an unusual word in the essay.
  • “Which source gave you this idea? Can you show me where?”

Students who wrote their own work can usually talk about it easily, even if they are nervous. Students who didn’t often can’t explain their own key sentences.

3. Check the sources

Chatbots sometimes invent sources: real-sounding books, articles or quotes that don’t exist, or real authors with made-up titles. Pick two or three references and look them up. Check that quoted passages appear in the source. A reference that can’t be found is a fair question to ask the student about.

In DraftTrail, students add sources and cite them as they write, and pasted passages they mark as quotes are listed separately, which makes this check quick.

4. Compare with writing done in class

A short piece written in class, on paper or in a monitored writing task, shows what a student can do on their own. If a take-home essay is very different in vocabulary, structure and accuracy, that’s worth a conversation. Our guide to running an in-class writing task on phones shows one way to do this with the devices students already have.

Design assignments that are hard to outsource

  • Make it personal or local. Ask students to connect the topic to their own experience, family, town or a class discussion.
  • Use your own materials. A graph from your lesson, a case you wrote, or a reading only your class has is hard for a chatbot to use well.
  • Grade the process. Give points for the outline, the draft and the reflection, not only the final text.
  • Ask for a short reflection: what was hardest, what changed between drafts, which source was most useful.

When you have real doubts

  1. Collect the evidence: process records, sources you checked, in-class work.
  2. Talk with the student privately, and listen.
  3. Follow your school’s academic honesty policy. If the evidence isn’t clear, give the student a chance to show their understanding, for example by rewriting a section in class.

Be clear with students from the start about what AI use is allowed in your class. Many teachers allow it for brainstorming or checking grammar, but not for writing the text. Clear rules make honest choices easier.
