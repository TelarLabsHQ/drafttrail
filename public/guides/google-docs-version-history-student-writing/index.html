How to Use Google Docs Version History to Check Student Writing

Every Google Doc keeps a history of how it changed. For teachers, it’s a free, built-in way to see how a piece of writing was made.

What you need

You need edit access to the document. Version history isn’t available with view or comment access. In Google Classroom, if you create the assignment with Make a copy for each student, you have edit access to each student’s copy automatically. Otherwise, ask students to share the doc with you as an Editor.

Open version history

  1. Open the student’s document.
  2. Choose File → Version history → See version history. The keyboard shortcut is Ctrl + Alt + Shift + H on Windows and ChromeOS, or ⌘ + Option + Shift + H on a Mac.
  3. A panel opens on the right with a list of versions, newest first, each with a date and time.
  4. Click a version to see the document as it was at that moment.
  5. Turn on Show changes at the bottom of the panel to highlight what was added at each step. Each person who edited gets a color.
  6. Some versions are grouped. Click the small arrow next to a version to expand it into more detailed versions.

What to look for

PatternWhat it may mean
Many versions over several days, with text growing and changingA normal writing process.
Whole paragraphs appearing in a single version, with no smaller steps beforeText that may have been pasted in. Worth asking where it came from: notes, another doc, or another source.
The first version already contains most of the essayThe text was written somewhere else and brought into this doc. Ask to see the original document.
Very few versions for a long essay, all within a few minutesFast work, or copied text. Look at the timestamps and talk with the student.
Edits made by another person’s colorSomeone else edited the document. That may be fine (a peer review) or not, depending on your rules.

Limits of version history

  • It groups edits. Versions are snapshots, not every keystroke. A paragraph typed in two minutes and a paragraph pasted in one second can look the same.
  • It doesn’t say “pasted”. You can see that text appeared, not how it got there.
  • Writing somewhere else breaks it. If a student writes in another app and copies the result in, the history starts with the finished text. That is a signal in itself, but it’s also how some honest students work, for example writing offline on a phone first.
  • It’s tied to the doc. A student who makes a fresh copy of the document starts a new history.

Use what you see as a reason to talk with the student, not as proof. Many honest students paste from their own notes or from a draft written on another device.

When you need more detail: DraftTrail

DraftTrail is a free writing page that records every edit inside its writing box, with the time. Compared with version history, it:

  • separates typed text from pasted text, and shows exactly what was pasted and when;
  • counts active writing time, sessions, thinking pauses and corrections;
  • lets you replay the essay from the first word, faster than real time, with pasted text highlighted;
  • lets students cite sources and mark quotes, so honest quotations aren’t mistaken for copying.

Students hand in a small .drafttrail file with their work, and you open many files at once on the review page. Nothing is uploaded unless you use the live in-class mode. Set up a task on the set-up page.

Tell students in advance

Let students know that you may look at how their work was written, and why: it helps you see their effort and protects them if anyone doubts their work. Students who know their process matters tend to keep drafts and notes, which is a good writing habit anyway.
