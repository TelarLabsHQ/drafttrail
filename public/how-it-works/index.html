How DraftTrail works

Every piece of writing leaves a trail: first words, pauses, rewrites, and sometimes pasted text. DraftTrail records that trail so it can be replayed.

What is recorded

  • Each change in the DraftTrail writing box: what was typed or deleted, where, and when.
  • Whether text was typed or pasted (or dropped in), and which pasted passages the student marked as quotes from a source.
  • The sources the student added and where they cited them.
  • How many times the student left the page while writing, and for how long.

What is not recorded

  • Anything outside the DraftTrail writing box: other apps, websites, the screen or the camera.
  • Nothing is sent to a server. The recording is saved on the student's device and in the .drafttrail file they download.

What the teacher sees

  • Flags to look at: large pastes, a high share of pasted text, very few corrections, unusually fast typing, a long essay in very little time, leaving the page often, or a file whose text doesn't match its own recording.
  • Numbers: words, % pasted, active writing time, sessions, revisions, thinking pauses.
  • A chart of the essay's length over time with paste markers, and a replay with pasted text highlighted.

What each number means

  • Active writing time adds up the time between edits, but counts any gap as at most 30 seconds. A student who leaves the essay open overnight doesn’t look like they wrote for 12 hours.
  • Sessions: a new session starts after a break of more than 5 minutes. Real essays are often written in two or three sittings.
  • Thinking pauses: gaps of more than 5 seconds between edits. Writers pause to plan, reread and choose words, so a long essay with almost no pauses is unusual.
  • Revisions: how much of the typed text was later deleted. Most people delete and rewrite as they go.
  • % pasted: how much of the final essay arrived by pasting or dropping text in, rather than typing. Pasted text the student marked as a quote is counted separately.

When a flag appears

FlagShown when
Large pasteA single paste of 15 words or more that the student didn’t mark as a quote.
High share of pasted text30% or more of the final text was pasted.
Lots of quoted text25% or more of the final text is quoted from sources. Quoting is allowed, but an essay that is mostly quotes may need a conversation.
Very few correctionsAn essay of 120 words or more where less than 2% of the typed text was deleted.
Unusually fast typingMore than 90 words per minute of active time.
Long essay, little time150 words or more written in less than 5 minutes of active time.

Flags are deliberately simple so you can explain them to a student or a parent in one sentence. None of them decides anything on its own.

Quotes and sources

Students can add sources (books, articles, web pages and more) and cite them while they write. DraftTrail formats the citations in APA 7, MLA 9 or Chicago, and builds the reference list. When a student pastes a passage and marks it as a quote from a source, it’s shown as a quote, not as an unexplained paste. This teaches the habit you want: copy only what you cite, and say where it came from.

Reading a replay

The replay shows the essay being written from the first word, faster than real time, with pasted text highlighted. Look for the shape of the work rather than single moments:

  • Usual: an outline or a rough start, then sentences that grow, move and get rewritten, with pauses in between.
  • Worth asking about: a whole paragraph arriving at once, or steady typing from start to finish with no pauses or corrections, which can mean the text was copied by hand from somewhere else.

Limits to know

  • A student could type out text from another source by hand. Replay often shows this as steady typing with no pauses or corrections.
  • Tech-savvy students could try to edit a .drafttrail file. DraftTrail checks that the final text matches its recording and flags it if not.
  • Flags are reasons to talk with the student, not proof.
