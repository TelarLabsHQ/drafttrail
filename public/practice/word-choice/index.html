    Why these words get mixed up

    Most confused words sound alike, look alike, or both. Spell checkers don’t catch them, because each word is spelled correctly. It’s just the wrong word. That’s why they slip into essays, emails and even published papers, and why readers notice them.

    The good news: there are only a few dozen common pairs, and each one has a simple rule. Learn the rule, practice it a few times, and the mistake usually disappears for good.

    Commonly confused words: a quick guide

    Affect / effect

    Affect is usually a verb: stress can affect sleep. Effect is usually a noun: the effect of stress. Memory trick: A for action (affect), E for end result (effect).

    Then / than

    Than compares (bigger than). Then is about time or order (first this, then that).

    Fewer / less

    Fewer for things you can count (fewer pages). Less for amounts (less time, less money).

    Its / it’s

    It’s = it is or it has. Its = belonging to it. If you can’t say “it is”, write its.

    Their / there / they’re

    Their = belonging to them. There = a place. They’re = they are.

    Your / you’re

    You’re = you are. Your = belonging to you.

    Lose / loose

    Lose = to misplace or not win. Loose = not tight.

    Principal / principle

    Principal = head of a school, or main. Principle = a rule or value.

    Complement / compliment

    Complement = completes or goes well with. Compliment = praise. Trick: a complement completes.

    i.e. / e.g.

    e.g. = for example (one of several). i.e. = that is (exactly this). Trick: e.g. = “example given”.

    Accept / except

    Accept = receive or agree. Except = not including.

    Advice / advise

    Advice is the noun (good advice). Advise is the verb (I advise you).

    Comprise / consist of

    Comprise already includes “of”: the course comprises five modules, or consists of five modules. Avoid “comprises of”.

    Lead / led

    The past tense of the verb lead is led. (Lead, pronounced “led”, is also a metal, which causes the confusion.)

    Who / whom

    Who does the action (who wrote this?). Whom receives it (to whom it may concern). If “him” fits, use whom.

    Imply / infer

    The speaker or writer implies. The listener or reader infers.

    Between / among

    Between for two. Among for three or more in a group.

    Farther / further

    Farther for physical distance. Further for more or additional (further study).

    Ensure / insure

    Ensure = make sure. Insure = protect with insurance.

    Could have / could of

    Always could have, should have, would have. “Could of” comes from how “could’ve” sounds.

    Peak / peek / pique

    Peak = top. Peek = quick look. Pique = stir up (it piqued my interest).

    Weather / whether

    Weather = rain and sun. Whether = if, or a choice between options.

    Elicit / illicit

    Elicit = draw out (a response). Illicit = illegal or not allowed.

    Cite / site / sight

    Cite = reference a source. Site = a place or website. Sight = vision.

    Discreet / discrete

    Discreet = careful and private. Discrete = separate and distinct.

    Stationary / stationery

    Stationary = not moving. Stationery = paper and envelopes (e for envelope).

    How to catch them in your own writing

    • Read your draft out loud. Swap in the long form: if “it is” or “you are” fits, use the apostrophe.
    • Search for your problem words. If you often mix up affect and effect, use Find (Ctrl+F or ⌘F) and check each one.
    • Keep a personal list. Write down the pairs you miss in this game and check for them before you submit.
    • Proofread after a break. Fresh eyes catch more than a fifth re-read right after writing.

    Questions

    Is “effect” ever a verb? Yes, in “to effect change”, meaning to bring it about. And “affect” can be a noun in psychology. Those uses are rarer, so the everyday rule is a good start.
