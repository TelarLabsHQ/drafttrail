What the readability scores mean

The checker uses two well-known formulas based on the length of your sentences and the number of syllables in your words. Longer sentences and longer words make text harder to read.

  • Flesch–Kincaid grade level estimates the US school grade needed to understand the text. A score of 8 means an average 8th-grade student (about age 13 to 14) should follow it.
  • Flesch reading ease runs from 0 to 100. Higher is easier: 60 to 70 is plain English, 30 or below is very hard.

Find the long sentences

Below the scores, the checker shows your text with long sentences highlighted: yellow for 25 to 34 words and red for 35 or more. Long sentences aren’t wrong, but too many in a row make writing tiring to read. Try splitting one into two, or turning a list inside a sentence into bullet points.

How to make writing easier to read

  • One idea per sentence. If a sentence has three commas and an “and”, it may be two sentences.
  • Prefer short, common words. Use instead of utilize, help instead of facilitate.
  • Mix sentence lengths. A short sentence after two long ones gives the reader a rest. Like this.
  • Use active voice. “The class planted the trees” is clearer than “The trees were planted by the class”.
  • Know your reader. A science report for your teacher can be harder than a newsletter for parents.

For teachers

Use the checker to match reading passages and instructions to your students’ level, or have students compare the reading level of two drafts. Readability formulas only measure sentence and word length; they don’t judge ideas, accuracy or style, so use them as one guide among many.

Want to see how a piece of writing was built, not just how it reads? Try writing in DraftTrail, and count your words with the word counter.

Questions

Does it work for other languages? The formulas are designed for English. Scores for other languages won’t be accurate.

Is my text private? Yes. Everything runs in your browser; nothing is uploaded.
